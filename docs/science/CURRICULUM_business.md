# Heurēka! business microgames: money, personal finance, accounting, startups and venture capital, business practice, economics

Compiled 2026-11-05. This file maps the units of widely used business and economics courses to WarioWare-style microgames for Heurēka!, the second app ("the everything else of STEM, plus business"). It follows the final microgame format (one shouted verb, one action of 3 to 6 seconds, the idea happening on screen, a funny fail). The course and unit structure follows the math app's docs/CURRICULUM.md.

## How to read this file

- **A microgame is a moment, not a question.** The game shouts one verb ("POUR!", "DILUTE!", "HOLD ON!"). The player does one action they can read in under a second (tap, mash, hold, swipe, drag, stop at the right moment). Success makes the idea happen on screen, and the idea is the reason the action works: the investors' bucket fills before any money reaches the founders, because they have a liquidation preference. Failure is funny and shows the same idea from the other side. The card shown afterwards says the idea in plain words.
- **No calculation and no formulas during play.** Amounts appear only as things the player can see and compare: a taller stack, a fuller jar, a thinner slice. During play the player reads at most three words. Terms such as "APR" or "liquidation preference" appear on the card, after the player has seen what they mean.
- **Order follows usefulness at work.** Courses, units and rows are ordered by how much the idea matters to people who run a household, a team, a company or a project. Reading financial statements, pricing, cash flow, hiring costs and fundraising come early. Topics that matter mainly for exams are marked **[TRIMMED]** and keep one short game. Practical topics that standard courses skip (negotiation, pricing tactics, cash timing, hiring costs, sales pipelines, estimation) have their own course, "Business practice".
- **"Why it matters"** under each unit heading says where people use the unit's ideas in real work.
- **Columns** (one row per microgame):
  - **ID**: a stable reference (EL = elementary, PF = personal finance, AC = accounting, SV = startups and venture capital, BP = business practice, MI = microeconomics, MA = macroeconomics).
  - **Command**: a verb the player does, one to three words, in capitals, 15 characters or fewer, ending in "!".
  - **Concept**: the one plain sentence the player comes to see.
  - **Scene**: what is on screen in the first second. It shows what the thing is before the player acts.
  - **Action**: the one thing the player does.
  - **What happens**: success, with the idea made visible.
  - **Funny fail**: the common wrong move, what it looks like, and what it shows.
  - **Level 2**: the same action made harder, or one new twist. Never a calculation.
  - **Host**: the animal that acts it out (frog, ant, penguin, duck, turtle, octopus, owl, fox, rabbit, beaver, hedgehog, bird).
- **The clarity bar.** A young child who has never seen the topic should be able to follow what happens on screen and know what to do, even in the venture-capital games.
- **Recurring pictures**, so a player who learns one game can read the next:
  - **The pie** is ownership of a company. Each slice belongs to someone. New investors add a slice; every other slice gets thinner, and the pie may get bigger.
  - **The tank** is a bank account. Money in pours from a tap at the top; money out drains from a tap at the bottom. The waterline is the cash on hand.
  - **The jars** are a budget: one labelled jar for each kind of spending.
  - **The scale** is the accounting equation: what a company has on one pan; what it owes, plus what belongs to its owners, on the other. It always balances.
  - **The doors** are a customer funnel: animals walk through a row of doors, and some turn back at each one.
  - **The buckets** show who gets paid first: money pours into the top bucket, and only its overflow reaches the next one.
- **[TRIMMED]** marks a topic that real courses teach but that matters mainly for exams; it keeps one short game.
- **[WEAK FIT]** marks a topic that fits no action well. This file uses it only as a last resort, and each such row still gives the best playable idea.
- **"See X"** means the idea is already taught by an earlier row in this file or by the math app (docs/CURRICULUM.md, for example G6-05 PERCENT!, A1-19 GROWTH FACTOR!, S-14 EXPECTED VALUE!). The earlier game is reused instead of a new one being added.
- **Amounts are realistic** where the card quotes them. Tax brackets on cards are the IRS figures for tax year 2026, and startup cards follow the conventions of Y Combinator's Post-Money SAFE User Guide. Where an amount is made up for the example, the row says "illustrative".

## Elementary (money basics for young children)

Source: no single elementary money course is standard, so the units combine two published frameworks. The Common Core math standard 2.MD.C.8 (fetched from thecorestandards.org) reads "Solve word problems involving dollar bills, quarters, dimes, nickels, and pennies, using $ and ¢ symbols appropriately." The National Standards for Personal Financial Education (Council for Economic Education and Jump$tart Coalition, released 2021-10-21; the six area names were verified in their press release) name six areas: earning income, spending, saving, investing, managing credit, managing risk. The early-grade benchmarks inside those standards were not fetched, so the unit titles below are reconstructed from the six area names and from the basic economics taught in early-grade social studies (goods and services, trade, choices).

These games use pictures and actions only, with no reading during play. Coins are drawn as US coins, each with a ring of tiny dots showing what it is worth (a nickel carries 5 dots, a dime 10, a quarter 25), so a player can see a coin's worth without knowing it.

## Personal finance (Khan Academy: "Financial Literacy"; Next Gen Personal Finance: "Semester Course")

Source: Khan Academy's Financial Literacy course lists 16 units (Welcome; Budgeting and saving; Consumer credit; Financial goals; Loans and debt; Insurance; Investments and retirement; Scams & fraud; Careers and education; Taxes and tax forms; Employment; Banking; Car buying; Housing; Teacher resources; Additional resources). All 16 titles were verified on Capital One's page about the course; "Budgeting & Saving" and "Consumer Credit" also appear in KA's blog post of 2022-12-19, and "Employment", "Banking", "Car buying" and "Housing" in KA's blog post of 2024-07-01. NGPF's Semester Course page (fetched) lists 11 units with their lessons: Behavioral Economics, Banking, Investing, Types of Credit, Managing Credit, Paying for College, Career, Insurance, Taxes, Budgeting, Consumer Skills. The interest units also draw on KA's "Finance and capital markets" course, whose sections "Interest and debt", "Inflation", "Stocks and bonds" and "Investment vehicles, insurance, and retirement" were seen on a copy of the course outline. The units below merge the two courses and are ordered by everyday importance. KA's welcome, teacher and resource units have no content and are not mapped; "Careers and education" and NGPF's "Career" and "Paying for College" are covered by the paycheck, student-loan and job-offer games (PF-17, PF-23, BP-13).

## Startups and venture capital (Y Combinator Startup School; YC Post-Money SAFE User Guide; standard venture texts)

Source: no single standard course covers this material, so the units were assembled from these sources. Y Combinator's Startup School 2018 curriculum (19 lectures, fetched from YC's blog) includes "How to Measure Your Product", "How to Get Users and Grow", "Fundraising Fundamentals" and "Safes, Notes, Equity"; YC's Startup Library adds "Startup pricing 101" (Kevin Hale; page fetched) and "Key Startup Metrics" (Tom Blomfield; title seen in search results, not fetched). SAFE mechanics follow YC's Post-Money SAFE User Guide (fetched; covers form versions 1.0 of 2018-09-28 to 1.2 of February 2023), including its rule that the SAFE's ownership is the money invested divided by the post-money valuation cap, and that a SAFE is diluted by an option-pool increase made in the priced round. The option-pool shuffle follows Venture Hacks' "The Option Pool Shuffle" (2007, fetched). Growth benchmarks follow Paul Graham's "Startup = Growth" (2012, fetched: 5–7% a week is a good rate during YC, and 5% a week compounds to about 12.6× in a year), and the runway game follows his "Default Alive or Default Dead?" (2015, fetched). The power-law game follows Correlation Ventures' study of over 21,000 financings from 2001 to 2013, as reported by Seth Levine (2014, fetched): 65% of financings returned less than the money invested and 4% returned 10× or more. The A/B-test games follow Evan Miller's A/B testing tools and his essay on peeking (titles seen, not fetched). The rule of thumb "a customer's lifetime value should be at least 3 times what it cost to win them" is widely quoted (for example in David Skok's "SaaS Metrics 2.0", not fetched) and is called a rule of thumb on the card. All 12 unit groupings below are reconstructed by this file, not taken from one course.

## Self-review

After the draft was complete, every row was checked against the owner's tests: the player does the key step with their own hands; the goal is obvious from the command and the first frame; one main picture changes; a satisfying success; a vivid fail that shows the idea; plain words a newcomer can't misread. The file went through four drafts as the owner's rules changed, and these are the rewrites that came out of the reviews:

- **The whole file (all rows).** The first two drafts were Q.E.D.!-style calculation games ("$2M post-money SAFE at an $8M cap: what does the holder own?"). The owner's "concepts only" rule and the final microgame format replaced every one of them with a verb, a 3–6 second action and an on-screen result. Calculations that carried no concept of their own were dropped: hourly pay to yearly salary, unit-price division, the APR formula, the loan-payment formula, depreciation arithmetic, the cash-budget sum, variance arithmetic, IRR, earnings per share, anti-dilution formulas, the A/B sample-size formula, GDP by expenditure, and unemployment-rate division.
- **Every guess-then-watch row.** The third draft had many Predict rows ("Who has more at 65?", "Will the tank go below its floor?"). The owner ruled out questions, so each became a goal the player achieves: PF-09 `PLANT EARLY!` (mash to plant coins while young), PF-11 `STAY ABOVE!` (shop without sinking the tank), PF-33 `TIP THE SEESAW!` (lift the duck by lowering rates), SV-17 `KEEP TESTING!` (hold the rope until the bars settle), and so on.
- **EL-01 and EL-12.** Both were "tap the right coin / tap one" choices. They now ask the player to fill the price's dot-holes, so the small dime filling the holes is a discovery made while acting.
- **AC-25 `SHARE OUT!`.** Its fail described the level-2 twist instead of a player mistake; it now shows a greedy ant toppling under a double pile.
- **MI-20, MI-25, MI-29, MA-16, MA-18, MA-23.** These had no funny fail; each now has one that shows the idea (for example MI-20: leaving one small stall free forces the monopolist's price back down).
- **MA-17 `LOOSEN UP!`.** It was "let go of the ball and watch", where the player mostly watched; the player now loosens the wage and price springs that let the economy settle.
- **MA-03 `PULL THE LEVER!`.** Its action text was garbled; it is now "Drag the lever up."
- **Repeated or overlong commands.** Seven commands were used twice (for example `POUR!`, `PLUG!`, `CATCH!`, `HIRE!`, `KEEP UP!`, `NEEDS FIRST!`) and eleven were longer than 15 characters (for example `MEET IN THE MIDDLE!`, `GRAB AN UMBRELLA!`). Each was renamed; every command is now unique and 15 characters or fewer.
- **Commands that were names or questions.** `WHO DECIDES!` and `WHAT'S MISSING!` read as questions and became `SEAT THE BOSS!` and `FISH OUT!`; first-draft names such as `NPV!`, `CAC!` and `EPS!` became verbs (`WALK BACK!`, `WIN CUSTOMERS!`, `SHARE OUT!`).
- **Prose claims.** Unbacked words such as "most" and "every" in "Why it matters" lines were replaced with claims the sources support (for example "salaries are often the largest cost for startups and service firms").

Rows that still need a playtest more than a rewrite: SV-34 `SOFTEN!` and SV-37 `ONE DIP!`, whose term-sheet ideas are the hardest to show without words; MI-25 `RAISE A FLOOR!`, whose card must state that the evidence on minimum wages is debated; and BP-16 `BREAK IT DOWN!`, which needs a scene where the small guesses are visibly easier than the big one.
