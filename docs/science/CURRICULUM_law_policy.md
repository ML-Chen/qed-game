# Heurēka! microgame map: law, government and policy, consulting and structured problem solving

Compiled 2026-11-05. This file maps the important ideas of law, government and policy, and structured problem solving to WarioWare-style microgames for Heurēka!. It follows the final microgame format and the layout of CURRICULUM_business.md.

## How to read this file

- **A microgame is a moment, not a question.** The game shouts one verb. The player does one action they can read in under a second (tap, hold, swipe, drag, stop at the right moment), and the action takes 3 to 6 seconds. Success makes the idea happen on screen: the debt letters bounce off the company's bubble because an LLC keeps business debts away from the owner's house. Failure is funny and shows the same idea from the other side. The card shown afterwards says the idea in plain words.
- **No questions, no calculations, no reading during play.** During play the player reads at most three words. Legal terms such as "consideration" or "due process" appear on the card, after the player has seen what they mean.
- **Order follows usefulness.** Courses, units and rows are ordered by how much the idea matters to people at work and in projects: contracts and intellectual property come before court structure; incentives and policy tools come before the details of the legislative process; communicating an answer and prioritizing come before frameworks.
- **"Why it matters"** under each unit heading says where people use the unit's ideas in real work.
- **Law cards: US law by default.** Every card in the Law course describes US law unless it says otherwise, and every Law card ends with the line "General information about US law, not legal advice." Each Law unit has an "Other countries" line saying in one sentence where other systems differ.
- **Government cards are even-handed.** Games show mechanisms and trade-offs. No game or card takes a side on a contested political question. Where the evidence is debated, the card says so ("studies disagree about how large this effect is").
- **Columns** (one row per microgame):
  - **ID**: a stable reference (EC = elementary, LW = law, GP = government and policy, PS = problem solving).
  - **Command**: a verb the player does, one to three words, in capitals, 15 characters or fewer, ending in "!".
  - **Concept**: the one plain sentence the player comes to see.
  - **Scene**: what is on screen in the first second.
  - **Action**: the one thing the player does.
  - **What happens**: success, with the idea made visible.
  - **Funny fail**: the common wrong move, what it looks like, and what it shows.
  - **Level 2**: the same action made harder, or one new twist. Never a calculation.
  - **Host**: the animal that acts it out.
- **The clarity bar.** A young child who has never seen the topic should be able to follow what happens on screen and know what to do, even in the law and policy games.
- **Recurring pictures**, so a player who learns one game can read the next:
  - **The scroll with a wax seal** is a contract. The seal clicks on only when the deal is complete.
  - **The owl judge** with a gavel decides disputes. The court ladder has three rungs: trial court, appeals court, Supreme Court.
  - **Fences** are intellectual property: a fence around your drawing (copyright), a fence with a countdown clock around an invention (patent), a badge on a product (trademark), a locked safe (trade secret).
  - **The bubble** is a company's liability shield: business debts bounce off it.
  - **The two-pan scale** weighs costs against benefits, or evidence in court.
  - **Ballots** are cards with picture stickers; a ranked ballot carries 1, 2, 3 stickers.
  - **Eye dots** show where an audience is looking.
- **"See X"** means the idea is already taught by another row, in this file or in CURRICULUM_business.md (for example MI-26 `CLEAN THE AIR!` for externalities, BP-16 `BREAK IT DOWN!` for estimation).
- **[TRIMMED]** marks a topic courses teach that matters mainly for exams; it keeps one short game.

## Elementary (rules, fairness and working together)

Source: the C3 Framework for Social Studies State Standards (National Council for the Social Studies), civics indicators for the early grades, fetched from a standards listing. They include "Explain the need for and purposes of rules in various settings inside and outside of school" (D2.Civ.3.K-2), "Describe democratic principles such as equality, fairness, and respect for legitimate authority and rules" (D2.Civ.8.K-2), "Explain how people can work together to make decisions in the classroom" (D2.Civ.11.K-2), "Explain what governments are and some of their functions" (D2.Civ.5.K-2) and "Compare their own point of view with others' perspectives" (D2.Civ.10.K-2). The unit titles below are this file's own. Units 4 and 6 are early versions of contracts, intellectual property and problem solving, which the C3 Framework doesn't cover.

These games use pictures and actions only, with no reading during play.

## Law (US law, with one line per unit on other countries)

Source: the University of Virginia School of Law's descriptions of its required first-year courses (fetched): Contracts ("legal requirements for enforceable contracts, including consideration, consent, and conditions"), Torts ("liability for intentional wrongdoing, negligence, and liability without fault, or strict liability"), Constitutional Law ("Judicial review, federalism, congressional powers and limits"), Criminal Law, Civil Procedure, Property, and Legal Research and Writing. Law schools teach these first; working people use contracts, intellectual property, business entities, employment and privacy law more often, so those units come first here. Units 2 to 6 are not 1L courses; their content comes from the agency and court sources named in each unit. Property and Civil Procedure are left out except for appeals and settlement.

Every card in this course ends with: "General information about US law, not legal advice."

### Unit 1: Contracts (1L Contracts)
Why it matters: jobs, leases, freelance work, software terms and sales all run on contracts, and a person who knows when a promise binds can avoid both bad deals and lawsuits.
Other countries: civil-law countries such as France and Germany don't use the consideration rule, so a serious promise can bind without anything given in return.

### Unit 2: Intellectual property (copyright, patents, trademarks, trade secrets, licences, who owns the work)
Why it matters: writers, designers, coders and founders make things worth owning, and an employer, client or investor will check who owns them.
Other countries: copyright arises automatically in most countries (the Berne Convention); patents and trademarks are granted country by country; Europe has no general fair-use rule; the UK gives copyright to some computer-generated works.

## Consulting and structured problem solving

Source: there is no single standard course. McKinsey's article "How to master the seven-step problem-solving process" (fetched), with Charles Conn and Robert McLean, authors of *Bulletproof Problem Solving* (2018), describes the steps: define the problem, disaggregate it with logic trees, prioritize, plan the work, analyse, synthesize, and tell the story. Hacking the Case Interview's issue-tree guide (fetched) defines an issue tree as "a visual diagram that breaks one large problem into smaller sub-problems", MECE as "mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive", the profit tree as "Profit = Revenue - Costs. Revenue = Price x Quantity", and recommends a hypothesis-driven approach and the 80/20 rule for choosing branches. PrepLounge's issue-tree guide (fetched) recommends an "answer-first" approach. ThinkInsights' summary of Barbara Minto's *The Pyramid Principle* (1985; fetched) gives the rule "Ideas at any level in the pyramid must always be summaries of the ideas grouped below them." Unit titles are this file's own. Market sizing by estimation is mapped in CURRICULUM_business.md (BP-16 `BREAK IT DOWN!`, BP-17 `WIGGLE!`, SV-18 `COUNT UP!`) and is not repeated.
